The three replay recommendations, explained

IAA hackathon, 20 October 2026. Backlog 3.9 (as-if replay), 3.11 (blending) and 3.12 (simulated books).

Two corrections before the explanations. Checking the numbers for this note turned up two problems in the replay, and both are now fixed.
  1. The replay still applied the bodily injury "floor" you removed from the model this morning. That floor is why the capped-severity options looked identical to the current method. The charts below are rerun without it.
  2. The pricing-model prior's 32% miss was a bug. For valuation dates partway through 2023, the prior used a full year of expected cost. Corrected, it performs the same as the simple prior.
As a result, recommendation 2 is withdrawn and recommendation 3 keeps its conclusion for a different reason.

What the replay does, in one paragraph

Pick a past date, for example 31 March 2023. Pretend it is that day: hide every claim reported later, refit the development patterns on what is left, and ask each method how much cost is still to be reported for the losses that have already happened. Then look at what actually arrived by 31 December 2024. A good method predicts the late claims well. The replay does this at five quarter-ends from December 2022 to December 2023. It can only hide claims by report date, because the claims file is a single snapshot. It cannot rewind case reserves.

1. Motor bodily injury: a severity decision, not a method decision

The problem

Bodily injury claims report late and stay open for a long time, and their case reserves run low on the youngest claims. A chain ladder on cost trusts those reserves, so it understates recent years. The pipeline therefore builds BI ultimate from two parts: the ultimate number of claims, which is reliable, times an average cost per claim taken from a mature year and trended forward. Everything then depends on how that average cost is chosen.

How much the choice moves the target

The capped options (B) put the 2024 BI ultimate 3% to 6% above the current method, rising with the trend rate. With base year 2022 instead of 2021, the range is 5% to 7%. BI is about 40% of motor cost, so this changes the motor level the model is anchored to.

What the replay says: it cannot tell A from B

The options differ by 1m to 3m a quarter in predicted late BI cost. One claim, a loss of 25 June 2023 reported on 16 December 2023 and valued at 11.9m, is larger than any of those differences. It sits inside the June and September bars as a late claim, then becomes known by December. No severity option predicts a single claim of that size, and none should. The replay therefore cannot rank A against B. On counts, which large claims do not distort, every method is within 3% of actual.

What the simulated books say: frequency x severity is right, Bornhuetter-Ferguson is not

The simulated books have a known true ultimate and reproduce the low BI case reserves. The current method lands within 1.5% of the truth. Bornhuetter-Ferguson, Cape Cod and Benktander are all about 14.5% low on every seed. Their prior is built from the cost triangle, so it inherits the low case reserves. That settles the method: keep frequency x severity for BI.

The simulation does not settle the severity option. Its BI severity was generated with the same 3.5% trend as option A, so A's close result is partly built in.

2. Buildings fire: recommendation withdrawn

What the replay said

Every method missed late-reported fire cost by about 90%, in the same direction. The replay recommended a credibility or capping treatment.

Why it does not matter

The 90% is a percentage of a very small number. Fire claims report quickly, so on average only 0.34m of fire cost was still to be reported at a valuation date. That is 2.4% of a year's fire ultimate of about 14.6m, and a handful of claims make up the whole amount. The methods predicted about twice the actual amount, which is about 0.3m, or 2% of a year's fire ultimate.

Recommendation: no change. Note in the report that fire's late reporting is too small to test. The fire trend you set this morning (4%) is unaffected.

3. Pricing-model prior: still not adopted, but for a different reason

The idea

Bornhuetter-Ferguson starts from a prior expected cost and replaces it with actual experience as claims develop. Backlog 3.3 suggested using the pricing model's own prediction as that prior, rather than a simple trended loss cost.

What went wrong in the first run

Corrected result

Corrected, the pricing-model prior predicts almost exactly what the simple prior does at every date. Neither beats the chain ladder outside BI: on motor other perils, buildings and contents, Bornhuetter-Ferguson moves the 2021 to 2024 ultimates by less than 0.2%.

Recommendation: do not adopt, because it changes nothing. The first run's "32% worse" was a bug and is withdrawn. The earlier explanation, that the prior inherited bias from the development pattern, was wrong.

4. The single large BI claim: should it just be removed?

The side effect we had not seen: it crowds out the other 2023 claims

Frequency x severity fixes each year's BI total at ultimate count x average cost, which is 51.3m for 2023. The pipeline then shares that total across the year's claims in proportion to their reported cost. The large claim reported 11.9m of 2023's 47.8m, so it takes 12.8m of the fixed 51.3m. That leaves only 38.6m for the other 2,052 claims, about 21% less per vehicle-year than the neighbouring years. The claim therefore does more than add its own size to 2023: it pushes down the developed cost of every other 2023 BI row.

Recommendation: cap, don't delete. Capping keeps the evidence that very large BI claims happen, as an expected load, and removes the distortion. It changes 2023 actuals, so fold A scores move. 2024 has no BI claim above 1m, so fold B actuals, which decide the final pick, stay the same. One owner run would measure it on fold B. If the gain clears the floor, it also answers section 1's severity question.

What this means for the decisions open today

  • The development method is confirmed for every peril group, and for BI the frequency x severity design is now supported by a known-truth test. Under the selection rule, the current target stands.
  • The only open target choice is BI severity. With the crowding-out found in section 4, capping the large claim in the target at 1m (option B applied to the target) is worth one owner run. If it doesn't clear the bar on fold B, keep option A and report B as a sensitivity.
  • One loose end: the target's BI actuals in ultimate.py still contain the floor, while the model's level no longer applies it. Both folds score against floored actuals, which are the same for every candidate, so model rankings are unaffected. It should be stated in the report.
